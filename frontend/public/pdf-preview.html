Viral Capsid Structure

Slide 3 EI 85%

Picture a d20 die — the one from D&D with 20 triangular faces. That's essentially what an icosahedral capsid looks like. It's the most common shape a virus uses to package its genetic material, and for good reason: it's geometrically perfect for fitting the maximum volume into the minimum surface area.

Each of those 20 faces is an equilateral triangle made of protein subunits called capsomeres. Drop them in a solution and they'll find each other and snap together like a puzzle — no cellular machinery, no ATP required. The shape is thermodynamically stable, which is a fancy way of saying it wants to be that shape.

Viral Replication Cycle

Slide 7 EI 92%

Think of a virus as a thief with two strategies. Strategy one: break in, raid everything, torch the place on the way out. That's the lytic cycle — the virus hijacks the cell's machinery, mass-produces copies, then lyses (bursts) the cell to release them. The cell dies, but hundreds of new viruses scatter to infect neighbours.

Strategy two: slip inside, blend in, wait. That's the lysogenic cycle. The viral DNA integrates into the host's genome and hitchhikes for generations without causing harm — until something stressful triggers it to cut loose and go lytic. The integrated viral DNA is called a provirus (in animal cells) or a prophage (in bacteria).
